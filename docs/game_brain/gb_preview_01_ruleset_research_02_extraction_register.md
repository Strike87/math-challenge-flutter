# R01 — Young, Bevan, & Sanders (2024)

### A. Bibliographic identity
Young, J. R., Bevan, D., & Sanders, M. (2024). *How Productive Is the Productive Struggle? Lessons Learned from a Scoping Review*. International Journal of Education in Mathematics, Science and Technology, 12(2), 470–495. DOI: `10.46328/ijemst.3364`. ERIC: `EJ1413403`.

### B. Research questions addressed
A — Productive challenge; B — Overchallenge; C — Underchallenge.

### C. Construct(s)
Productive struggle in mathematics education; challenge; effort; persistence; obstacles/mistakes; cognitive discomfort; instructional context.

### D. Population
K–12 mathematics-education literature involving students and/or teachers.

### E. Domain
Mathematics education.

### F. Setting
Primarily classroom/instructional research represented in the reviewed literature.

### G. Task
Engagement with challenging mathematical tasks or problems.

### H. Study design
Scoping review. The authors explicitly position the method as mapping an evolving, heterogeneous construct and identifying gaps rather than estimating one pooled causal effect.

### I. Operational measure(s)
No single uniform operational measure. The reviewed literature uses heterogeneous qualitative and quantitative representations of productive struggle, instructional practices, learner engagement, persistence, responses to obstacles/mistakes, and task context.

### J. Source-reported evidence requirements
The construct is tied to meaningful engagement with challenge and effort, not simply to a performance percentage. The review emphasizes variation in definitions and methods and notes that productive struggle should be distinguished from unproductive frustration.

### K. Source-reported threshold/predicate
`SOURCE-REPORTED EXACT THRESHOLD = NONE IDENTIFIED`

### L. Assumptions
Interpretation depends on an instructional/task context in which challenge is real, effort/persistence are meaningful, and the observed process can be distinguished from undue frustration.

### M. Main findings relevant to the construct
The review finds that the literature lacks a single settled operationalization and is methodologically heterogeneous. Many studies focus on teacher implementation rather than directly validated student-state measurement, and qualitative/non-empirical designs are common. Productive struggle is therefore not a simple synonym for low accuracy, slow responding, or error occurrence.

### N. Uncertainty / limitations
The review itself restricts scope to mathematics productive struggle and notes an evolving conceptual literature. Heterogeneous definitions and measures limit direct operational transfer. A scoping review maps the field but does not validate one universal measurement rule.

### O. Transfer risks
High. `PreviewEvidence` does not contain direct task-complexity, effort, persistence, instructional-design, teacher-support, frustration, or later-learning measures. Treating synthetic accuracy/latency/timeouts as productive struggle would collapse a multidimensional instructional construct into a performance proxy the source does not validate.

### P. Synthetic PreviewEvidence applicability

### Q. Evidence unavailable from PreviewEvidence
Direct task challenge/demand; effort/persistence; instructional support; learner engagement process; distinction between productive persistence and undue frustration; downstream learning/transfer; teacher/student contextual observations.

### R. Proposed abstention condition justified by this source
When only aggregate counts, derived accuracy, timeout counts, and a synthetic latency category are available without direct evidence of mathematical task demand and engagement-with-challenge, this source does not justify a productive-struggle interpretation.

### S. Source locator(s)
- Article pp. 470–495; abstract and introductory definition discussion near the opening pages.
- Methods section: scoping-review rationale, inclusion approach, and K–12 mathematics scope.
- Results/discussion: methodological heterogeneity and dominance of qualitative/non-empirical work.

### T. Extraction notes
The source is useful for construct boundaries and for warning against reducing productive struggle to a single performance marker. It does not supply a candidate project predicate.

# R04 — Pyke, Lunau, & Javadi (2025)

### A. Bibliographic identity
Pyke, W., Lunau, J., & Javadi, A.-H. (2025). *Does difficulty moderate learning? A comparative analysis of the desirable difficulties framework and cognitive load theory*. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 78(10), 2181–2195. DOI: `10.1177/17470218241308143`. PMCID: `PMC12432286`.

### B. Research questions addressed
A — Productive challenge; B — Overchallenge; C — Underchallenge.

### C. Construct(s)
Desirable difficulties; retrieval effort; task/item difficulty; cognitive load theory; intrinsic cognitive load; extraneous cognitive load; element interactivity; learner expertise/prior knowledge; delayed retention.

### D. Population
Theoretical comparison drawing across learning-research populations and task types.

### E. Domain
Cognitive/educational psychology and learning research.

### F. Setting
Experimental and instructional learning contexts discussed by the compared frameworks.

### G. Task
Learning tasks whose difficulty, retrieval demand, material complexity, feedback, or instructional presentation can vary.

### H. Study design
Comparative theoretical review/integration. The proposed reconciliation model is conceptual and explicitly requires further empirical validation.

### I. Operational measure(s)
The source discusses multiple non-equivalent measures and manipulations: retrieval difficulty, initial performance, later retention, task/item difficulty, material element interactivity, working-memory load, learner expertise, feedback, and instructional presentation. It explicitly distinguishes intrinsic cognitive load from item difficulty.

### J. Source-reported evidence requirements
Difficulty effects must be interpreted in relation to material complexity and learner expertise. Desirable-difficulty effects are normally evaluated through learning/retention outcomes, not only immediate performance. Cognitive-load interpretations require information about working-memory demands, element interactivity, expertise, and instructional design.

### K. Source-reported threshold/predicate
`SOURCE-REPORTED EXACT THRESHOLD = NONE IDENTIFIED`

### L. Assumptions

### M. Main findings relevant to the construct
The paper argues that desirable-difficulty and cognitive-load perspectives can appear contradictory because they often concern different materials, complexity levels, learner expertise, and outcomes. Effort is not itself a clean universal operational measure.

### O. Transfer risks

### R. Proposed abstention condition justified by this source

# R07 — Fagerland, Lydersen, & Laake (2015; online 2011)

### A. Bibliographic identity
Fagerland, M. W., Lydersen, S., & Laake, P. (2015). *Recommended confidence intervals for two independent binomial proportions*. Statistical Methods in Medical Research, 24(2), 224–254. DOI: `10.1177/0962280211415469`. First published online 2011-10-13.

### B. Research questions addressed
D — Recent improvement; E — Sparse evidence/abstention; G — Uncertainty.

### C. Construct(s)
Frequentist interval estimation for differences, ratios, and odds ratios of two independent binomial proportions; small-sample coverage behavior; method selection under different parameter/sample regimes.

### D. Population
Methodological/statistical; no learner population.

### E. Domain
Statistical inference for independent binomial samples.

### F. Setting
Two-sample binomial-proportion comparison.

### G. Task
Estimate interval uncertainty for differences/ratios/odds ratios between two independent binomial proportions.

### H. Study design
Peer-reviewed methodological comparison using analytical/simulation performance of confidence-interval procedures under many sample-size and probability configurations.

### I. Operational measure(s)
Two independent samples with binomial outcomes; sample sizes; observed successes/proportions; estimands such as difference, ratio, and odds ratio; interval coverage and related method-performance characteristics.

### J. Source-reported evidence requirements
The method comparison presumes binomial samples and independence between the two groups/samples. Recommended methods vary with estimand, sample size, probability region, and sample-size balance. Small-sample performance cannot be treated as method-independent.

### K. Source-reported threshold/predicate
`SOURCE-REPORTED THRESHOLD EXISTS`

These are author recommendations for confidence-interval procedures under the paper's two-independent-binomial-sample model; they are not project evidence-count requirements.

- **Difference of proportions:** for **less than 30 in each sample**, the prime recommendation is the **Agresti–Min exact unconditional interval**; Newcombe hybrid score and Agresti–Caffo are stated as alternatives. For **more than 30 in each sample**, the authors prefer the **Newcombe hybrid score interval**; Agresti–Caffo and Miettinen–Nurminen asymptotic score are described as similarly performing alternatives. If coverage must be at least nominal, they recommend Agresti–Min exact unconditional. Locator: Section 8.1, p. 26; Table 7, p. 26. Estimand: difference between two independent binomial proportions; interval criteria: coverage near nominal across parameter values, with short length preferred when coverage is similar (Section 8, p. 26).
- **Ratio of proportions:** for **40 or more subjects in each sample** and proportions **greater than 0.1**, the **inverse hyperbolic sine interval** is a stated alternative to Koopman asymptotic score; the authors do **not** recommend Katz log or adjusted log unless sample size is **at least 75 in each sample**. Locator: Section 8.3, p. 28. Estimand: ratio of two independent binomial proportions; interval criterion: coverage behavior, including unequal-sample/small-proportion limitations.
- **Odds ratio:** the **Baptista–Pike mid-p interval** is recommended ahead of Agresti–Min exact unconditional for **all sample sizes**; if unavailable or too computationally costly, Gart adjusted logit is recommended when the OR is not far from one, with source example **OR = 10** or **OR = 0.1** stated as not problematic. Locator: Section 8.4, p. 28; Table 7, p. 26. Estimand: odds ratio; interval criterion: coverage performance and availability.

**Empirical simulation/performance examples, not author recommendation statements:** Figure 1 evaluates the difference estimand at `n1+ = n2+ = 10` and `40` for fixed differences `0.2` and `0.3`; the text reports that the Wald interval does not generally reach approximately nominal coverage until `n1+ = n2+ = 100`. Locator: Section 4.2.4, pp. 7–8, Figure 1. These figures/results illustrate evaluated method behavior; they are not an evidence-sufficiency rule and must not be conflated with the Section 8 recommendations above.

`TRANSFER TO PREVIEWEVIDENCE = NOT DEFENSIBLE`

No reported sample-size recommendation is adopted as a GB-PREVIEW-01 evidence-sufficiency threshold.

### L. Assumptions
Independent samples; binomial sampling model; an explicit two-sample estimand; applicability of frequentist interval-performance criteria to the data-generating process.

### M. Main findings relevant to the construct
Naive large-sample/Wald-style intervals can perform poorly, especially at small samples and extreme proportions. No single procedure is uniformly optimal across all estimands and parameter regions; method performance depends materially on assumptions and sample configuration.

### N. Uncertainty / limitations
The paper is statistical-methodology guidance, not a semantic learner-interpretation framework. Its sample-size guidance concerns interval coverage under a specific model and cannot determine when a synthetic educational scenario is substantively interpretable.

### O. Transfer risks
Very high. PreviewEvidence does not establish independent binomial sampling, two independent comparable samples, a valid estimand, or a requirement to emit a confidence interval. Earlier/recent segments may be dependent observations from one synthetic context rather than independent samples.

### P. Synthetic PreviewEvidence applicability
`TRANSFER_NOT_DEFENSIBLE`

### Q. Evidence unavailable from PreviewEvidence
Independence/i.i.d. justification; a validated binomial sampling model; explicit two-sample estimand; sampling design; justification that segment comparisons meet the paper's inferential assumptions.

### R. Proposed abstention condition justified by this source
If independence, binomial modeling, and the relevant estimand are not established, do not import the paper's confidence-interval method recommendations or sample-size guidance into semantic scenario interpretation.

### S. Source locator(s)
- Reviewed public copy: University of Kentucky course-hosted publisher-formatted OnlineFirst PDF, `https://www.ms.uky.edu/~mai/sta635/FagerlandLydersenLaake2011---RecommendedCIsForTwoIndependent....pdf`; it bears `Statistical Methods in Medical Research 0(0)`, 1–31, © 2011 and is not identified as an author manuscript/preprint.

# R09 — Franc, Prusa, & Voracek (2023)

### A. Bibliographic identity
Franc, V., Prusa, D., & Voracek, V. (2023). *Optimal Strategies for Reject Option Classifiers*. Journal of Machine Learning Research, 24(11), 1–49. JMLR article `21-0048`.

### B. Research questions addressed
E — Sparse evidence/abstention; F — Conflict; G — Uncertainty; H — Precedence.

### C. Construct(s)
Reject-option/selective classification; cost-based rejection; bounded selective risk; bounded abstention/coverage; conditional expected risk; proper uncertainty score.

### D. Population
Theoretical/statistical classification; no learner population.

### E. Domain
Machine learning/statistical decision theory.

### F. Setting
Selective classification under an explicit probabilistic/loss model.

### G. Task
Choose a prediction or reject in order to optimize a defined risk/coverage objective.

### H. Study design
Peer-reviewed theoretical methods paper with formal derivations.

### I. Operational measure(s)
Input/label distribution, loss function, Bayes/conditional risk, selective risk, coverage/rejection rate, reject cost or target risk/coverage, uncertainty score.

### J. Source-reported evidence requirements
The formal strategies require a statistical model for `(X,Y)`, a loss/decision problem, and an objective such as reject cost, bounded selective risk, or bounded abstention. Optimal rejection is defined relative to conditional expected risk and target/cost parameters.

### K. Source-reported threshold/predicate
`SOURCE-REPORTED THRESHOLD EXISTS`

The following are formal source-level predicates under the paper's explicit probabilistic/loss model; symbols retain the paper's meanings and are not mapped to PreviewEvidence.

- **Cost-based:** with reject loss `epsilon`, the optimal Bayes selection function accepts when `r*(x) < epsilon`, may randomize with `tau in [0,1]` when `r*(x) = epsilon`, and rejects when `r*(x) > epsilon`; `r*(x)` is the minimal conditional expected risk. Locators: Section 2.1, Problem 1 and Eqs. (1)–(4), pp. 3–4.
- **Bounded-improvement:** given target selective risk `lambda > 0`, maximize coverage `phi(c)` subject to `R_S(h,c) <= lambda` (Problem 2, Eq. (7), p. 4; for fixed `h`, Problem 3, Eq. (8), p. 5). Theorem 2 defines the centered conditional risk `r̄(x) = r(x) - lambda`, `rho` and `b` (Eqs. (9)–(14), p. 5); Theorem 3 gives `gamma = b + lambda` and accepts for `r(x) < gamma`, randomizes at equality with `tau`, and rejects for `r(x) > gamma` (Eqs. (15)–(16), pp. 5–6).
- **Bounded-abstention:** given target coverage `0 < omega <= 1`, minimize selective risk `R_S(h,c)` subject to `phi(c) >= omega` (Problem 4, Eq. (17), p. 6; fixed-`h` Problem 5, Eq. (18), p. 6). Theorem 5 defines threshold `beta` from the target coverage (Eqs. (19)–(22), p. 7); Theorem 6 accepts when `r(x) < beta`, randomizes with `kappa` at equality, and rejects when `r(x) > beta` (Eqs. (23)–(24), p. 7).

The equality boundaries are part of the source predicates: cost-based equality permits arbitrary reject/accept without changing `R_B`; bounded-improvement equality uses `tau`; bounded-abstention equality uses `kappa`. The latter two randomize only as their cited equations specify.

`TRANSFER TO PREVIEWEVIDENCE = NOT DEFENSIBLE`

Do not translate `r(x)`, `epsilon`, `gamma`, `beta`, `lambda`, `omega`, risk, coverage, or reject cost into PreviewEvidence fields.

### L. Assumptions
Probabilistic data-generating model; finite label/decision setup; loss function; conditional expected risk; a specified objective/constraint; access to a suitable uncertainty/risk score.

### M. Main findings relevant to the construct
Several common reject-option formulations can be related under the paper's formal model, and a proper uncertainty score can support optimal rejection strategies. Forced classification is not intrinsically required when the decision problem permits rejection.

### N. Uncertainty / limitations
The result is conditional on an explicit classifier/risk framework. It does not say how to create a semantic educational scenario when the predictive target, probabilities, loss, and risk model do not exist.

### O. Transfer risks
Very high. PreviewEvidence does not contain class probabilities, a trained classifier, loss/reject cost, selective risk, target coverage, or a proper uncertainty score. Treating scenario precedence as a reject-option optimization would invent missing machinery.

### P. Synthetic PreviewEvidence applicability
`NOT_REPRESENTED`

### Q. Evidence unavailable from PreviewEvidence
Predictive target/classes; conditional probabilities; loss function; reject cost; selective risk; target coverage; proper uncertainty score; training/model estimation.

### R. Proposed abstention condition justified by this source
If no explicit predictive risk/loss/coverage model exists, do not use the paper's optimal-rejection thresholds or optimization results to choose among GB-PREVIEW-01 scenario hypotheses.

### S. Source locator(s)
- Official JMLR version-of-record PDF, `https://jmlr.org/papers/volume24/21-0048/21-0048.pdf`.
- Section 2.1, pp. 3–4: cost-based model, Problem 1, Eqs. (1)–(4), including the equality/randomization boundary.

# R10 — AERA, APA, & NCME (2014)

### A. Bibliographic identity
American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, & National Council on Measurement in Education. (2014). *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*. Washington, DC: AERA.

### B. Research questions addressed
A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H.

### C. Construct(s)
Validity of score interpretations and uses; reliability/precision; measurement error; classification/cut scores; documentation; fairness and appropriate interpretation.

### D. Population
General educational and psychological testing populations.

### E. Domain
Educational and psychological measurement standards.

### F. Setting
Any testing/assessment use for which score interpretations or decisions are claimed.

### G. Task
Establish and document evidence supporting intended interpretation/use of observed scores or measurements.

### H. Study design
Professional methodological standards jointly issued by AERA, APA, and NCME.

### I. Operational measure(s)
Validity evidence is not one numeric measure. Relevant evidence can include content, response processes, internal structure, relations to other variables, consequences/use, reliability/precision, and measurement-error information appropriate to the claimed interpretation and population. Cut-score use requires separate justification and precision around the decision region.

### J. Source-reported evidence requirements
An interpretation/use requires a validity argument supported by evidence and theory. Reliability/precision evidence must match intended score use, population, and conditions. Significant changes in procedures/conditions can require separate evidence. Classification/cut-score procedures require documented rationale and appropriate precision.

### K. Source-reported threshold/predicate
The Standards do not prescribe one universal score, reliability, evidence-count, or cut-score threshold for all purposes.

`SOURCE-REPORTED EXACT THRESHOLD = NONE IDENTIFIED`

### L. Assumptions
Interpretation validity is use-specific and population/context-specific. Measurement quality and decision precision must be demonstrated for the intended claim rather than inferred from face similarity of an observed variable.

### M. Main findings relevant to the construct
Observed scores/data do not automatically validate an intended interpretation. A validity argument can require revision, restriction, or further evidence when the proposed interpretation is under-supported. Reliability/precision evidence is contextual; there is no single preferred reliability index or universally optimal method. Cut scores combine technical/empirical considerations with judgment and must be justified rather than treated as self-validating constants.

### N. Uncertainty / limitations
The Standards are deliberately general and do not tell GB-PREVIEW-01 which scenario is true or what predicate to implement. They govern the evidentiary burden for making such interpretations.

### O. Transfer risks
Low for governance principles, high for any attempt to infer a concrete scenario rule. The Standards apply directly to the need for validity evidence, but they do not validate synthetic accuracy, timeout, or latency as measures of challenge/learning.

### P. Synthetic PreviewEvidence applicability
`PARTIALLY_REPRESENTED`

PreviewEvidence contains observable synthetic quantities, but the construct-validity link from those quantities to challenge/learning/uncertainty is not supplied.

### Q. Evidence unavailable from PreviewEvidence
Independent validity evidence; theory linking fields to the intended scenario construct; measurement-error/reliability evidence; external/criterion relations; response-process evidence; population/generalization evidence; decision precision for any proposed cut score.

### R. Proposed abstention condition justified by this source
When no validity argument and supporting evidence link a permitted PreviewEvidence field to the intended scenario interpretation/use, the substantive interpretation should be withheld rather than treated as established by the observed score/field alone.

### S. Source locator(s)
- Official stable open-access 2014 English PDF, linked by AERA: `https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf`.
- Chapter 1, Validity: Standard 1.0 and Standard 1.1, p. 23 (each intended score interpretation/use requires appropriate validity evidence; interpretation/use and intended population/construct must be specified).
- Chapter 2, Reliability/Precision and Errors of Measurement: Standard 2.0, p. 42; Standard 2.6, p. 44; Standard 2.13, p. 45 (appropriate reliability/precision evidence and non-interchangeability/measurement-error constraints).
- Chapter 5, Scores, Scales, Norms, Score Linking, and Cut Scores: Standard 5.0, p. 102; Standard 5.21, pp. 107–108 (cut-score rationale/procedures and required precision near a decision region).

### T. Extraction notes
This is the strongest Batch-01 source for epistemic governance, not for a project threshold. It supports caution, validity argumentation, and abstention where interpretation support is missing.
